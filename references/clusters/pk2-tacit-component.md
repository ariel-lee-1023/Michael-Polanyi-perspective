# Personal Knowledge, Part Two: The Tacit Component (chs. 5–7) [pk05–pk07]

**Register.** This is the book’s most capacious middle movement: developmental psychology and animal learning lead into language, mapping, mathematics, discovery, art, religion, cultural institutions, and the pathology of Marxist science. Its sentences are a little longer than the firsthand corpus norm (mean 27.96 words; median 26; stdev 14.91), more openly first-person (57.6%), and relatively forward in its assertion (4.74 boosters and 8.52 hedges per 1k; hedge:booster 1.80). Semicolons (3.88 per 1k) help it accumulate distinctions before a verdict. The address is philosophical but deliberately concrete: a chimpanzee and child, a maze, a bicycle, a map, an anatomist, a scientific controversy. It repeatedly takes an apparently impersonal accomplishment and restores the tacit performance, passion, and fellowship on which it rests.

**Ch. 5, Articulation.** Begin with the Gua–Donald comparison, not as a sentimental contrast between animal and man but as a staircase argument. The two develop in near parallel for a time; the child’s enormous later superiority comes when speech opens sustained thought and inheritance of a culture. The startling conclusion is that this result rests on an “almost imperceptible advantage” in original inarticulate powers. The argument must retain all three rungs: human intellectual superiority depends almost entirely on language; the capacity for language cannot itself be explained by language; and, with linguistic clues removed, human superiority on animal problems is slight. Articulation enlarges native powers enormously, but it never abolishes their tacit ground. The mute acts shared with the animal remain active inside even the most formal intelligence.

The chapter develops this conclusion through kinds of learning rather than by declaring a theory of mind at once. Trick-learning, sign-learning, and latent learning prefigure invention, observation, and interpretation. In each case distinguish the irreversible heuristic act from its reversible sequel: contriving from repeating a trick, observing from responding to a sign, understanding from routine problem-solving. At the articulate level, inventions and discoveries add to knowledge, whereas routine performances operate inside an established framework. This distinction licenses a recurring move: do not rate the performance of a fixed rule above the act that first saw the rule or reframed the situation in which it can work.

Language is then treated as an instrument with operational powers, but never as a self-sufficient code. Its three kinds of utterance—expression, appeal, and statement—keep interpersonal passion in view even while the analysis concentrates on indicative speech. Writing, mathematics, maps, diagrams, and pictures all enter under language insofar as they serve symbolic representation. The first operational power makes signs repeatable and consistently applicable; it supports memory, description, accumulated records, and exact naming. The second makes a reduced representation manipulable: the map ceases to help when brought to the scale of the territory, whereas a sketch can disclose the structural possibilities of a problem. The inventor’s sketchbook becomes a laboratory because a compact representation permits imaginative rearrangement. Articulation can therefore extend both memory and speculation without replacing judgment.

Keep the two services together, since the chapter refuses both a merely communicative and a merely computational picture of language. Printed records make it possible to revisit and rearrange knowledge from new points of view; libraries and concise books enlarge the available field of humanistic work as well as natural inquiry. A diagram, map, or formula also strips a situation to relations on which the imagination can operate. But the economy of representation is not its truth criterion. Its value consists in giving a knower more purchase on a subject without dictating what counts as the pertinent relation. The recurrent contrast is therefore not word versus world, or symbol versus reality, but a formal aid used competently versus the fiction that the aid has superseded the art of use.

This supports a distinctive treatment of science. Descriptive sciences enlarge ordinary speech by nomenclature and records; exact sciences use theories to bear on experience; deductive sciences increasingly work with objects generated by their own symbols. Yet the progression in formality is not a progression away from personal knowledge. Precision in denoting is an acquired discrimination akin to the taxonomist’s connoisseurship: “Denotation, then, is an art.” A richer vocabulary makes more exact discrimination possible, but it cannot provide a rule that decides every novel application. In a changing world, speaker and hearer must continually adjust text, conception, and experience; a new use may correct the text, alter the conception, reinterpret the experience, or be dismissed as meaningless. The decision is an act of responsible judgment, not a mechanical consequence of prior usage.

Use the tacit–articulate spectrum as this chapter’s internal map, but refer to `frameworks.md` for its compact definition. At one extreme is the “ineffable domain”: not a mysticism, but the ordinary case in which skill is known only in practice. The bicycle and the recognition of one’s macintosh show that a person can know perfectly well what they cannot specify, because the particulars are known instrumentally and not as focal objects. Maxims and diagnostic clues are useful, but their specification is not exhaustive. A second insufficiency arises when the particulars are available but their integration cannot be formalized: one may describe the elements of a complex topography without being able to state their spatial relations, so the learner must make the integrative act of insight without formal guidance. Animal maze-learning and anatomical exploration make the continuity of such understanding palpable.

At the middle of the spectrum, the tacit is “co-extensive with the text” that bears its meaning. Reading is not merely seeing marks or hearing sounds: focal attention is directed to the meaning, while the text and the history of learning its words remain subsidiary. This is why a text does not simply transfer its content like an object. It activates an acquired power of understanding. At the other extreme lies the “domain of sophistication,” in two opposed forms: clumsy speech can encumber thought, while formal operations may outrun present understanding and anticipate a new mode of thought. Do not turn this into a blanket anti-formalism. The point is that formalism can discipline, extend, and even lead thought forward, but its operation and its eventual appraisal remain inseparable from unspecifiable participation. Philosophies that refuse this description enforce “quixotic standards” of meaning that would issue in “voluntary imbecility.”

The closing movement of the chapter transfers the issue from language to frameworks. A valid framework may be a theory, a mathematical discovery, or a symphony; it works “by dwelling in it.” Astronomic observation is not first neutral seeing and then theory added from outside: the observer sees from within astronomic theory. Mathematics likewise has its established domain between drill and lone discovery, a domain in which the mathematician can lose himself in its contemplated greatness. Teaching must therefore convey not only results but the capacity for this contemplative indwelling. A framework may also have to be abandoned or transformed. Here the move is to make conversion structurally continuous with discovery: a new framework cannot be judged wholly from the outside, for it must first be inhabited enough to disclose what it can see.

**Ch. 6, Intellectual Passions.** This chapter forbids a detached account of intellectual value. Passion is neither subjective decoration added after inquiry nor a substitute for truth. It is the personal power that selects significant problems, recognizes coherence and beauty, sustains risk, and moves an audience toward a new framework. The argument is built from the distinction between routine and heuristic operations already made in chapter 5. A true discovery is not the endpoint of a strictly logical procedure; the obstacle it crosses is a “logical gap,” and “illumination” is the leap across it, a plunge toward “another shore of reality.” The scientist stakes professional life on such crossings. This is a high-hazard account of discovery, not a license for arbitrary novelty.

Use the three functions of intellectual passion separately. The selective function judges what deserves scientific attention and what counts as a real problem; it supplies the standards of relevance that no inventory of data yields by itself. The heuristic function powers the anticipatory search by which a problem is held to disclose something not yet known. The persuasive function becomes decisive where rival conceptual systems are logically separated. Formal operations within one framework cannot demonstrate its claims to those who rely on another; before they can understand its arguments, they must learn to speak and see within its terms. The proposer must win intellectual sympathy for a doctrine not yet grasped. Acceptance is therefore a self-modifying heuristic act—a conversion—which creates a school temporarily separated from outsiders by a logical gap.

The aesthetic passages should be used to show why scientific valuation has cognitive seriousness. A symphony demands recognition as something excellent; science and engineering face the additional resistance of reality, for no theory is beautiful if false and no invention ingenious if impracticable. Still, the scientist’s standards of value are set and judged through intellectual passion. The chapter’s claim is not that beauty verifies a theory by itself, but that science could not even identify or pursue its standards without a personal valuation which believes them to disclose reality. This gives the work a useful formulation for disputes over merely procedural accounts of research: expose the passionate criterion already at work, then ask whether it is directed toward reality or toward a substitute satisfaction.

The chapter broadens this analysis into morality and religion without making them mere extensions of laboratory method. Moral judgments are appraisals akin to intellectual valuations; culture involves affirming and dwelling in things that cannot be reduced to observable facts. The danger emerges when a mechanistic objectivism discredits open profession of moral passions. Those passions do not disappear; they can enter a political machine in disguised form. The later Marxism analysis is prepared here: the issue is not emotion against reason but whether avowed passions are disciplined by a reality-transcending standard or are converted into instruments of power.

**Ch. 7, Conviviality.** The social chapter completes the account of tacit knowing. An individual’s articulate power is inherited, trained, and maintained in company. Education gives the child command of a cultural heritage through inarticulate capacities activated by speech, print, and other symbols; the educated person is aware of a subject’s mastery while remaining focally aware of hardly any of its innumerable particulars. Tacit participation is thus not a private remainder left after communication. It is the condition under which communication means anything and under which a tradition can be transmitted, criticized, and renewed.

Treat culture as an achieved social order with four interdependent coefficients rather than as an undifferentiated consensus. The chapter names the sharing of convictions, the sharing of fellowship, co-operation, and authority or coercion. Universities, churches, theatres, and galleries principally embody culture; social intercourse, ritual, and common defence principally embody convivial loyalty; economic arrangements organize cooperation; public power shelters and controls these institutions. The distinctions matter because a society cannot be described merely as force, utility, or agreement. Intellectual life needs institutions that sustain shared standards while leaving their performance to persons capable of judgment.

This makes the critique of Marxism a diagnosis of an epistemic and civic mechanism. Polanyi grants its enormous persuasive force: it lets a morally troubled mind satisfy moral aspiration while claiming ruthless objectivity. Its negative branch reduces bourgeois ideals to bourgeois interests, turns that reduction into an unmasking, and keeps the unmaskers’ own moral motive undeclared. Its positive branch casts socialist aspiration as scientific fact. The result is a “self-confirmatory reverberation” between concealed passion and allegedly scientific assertion, named a “dynamo-objective coupling.” Criticism of the asserted science is met by passion; moral criticism is dismissed by the supposed verdict of science. The analysis neither treats moral passion as dispensable nor celebrates suspicion as such; it shows how the refusal to own a passion makes it politically irresistible.

The Lysenko episode supplies the terminal case. The formula that “all science is class science” turns scientific merit into party allegiance, so the old discrimination between truth and political utility is replaced by a partisan credential. This chapter’s point is not merely that a particular administration made bad decisions. Once science is required to serve the doctrine that authenticates it, the independent passions and standards by which scientists recognize and correct error are subordinated. Load this chapter when a discussion needs the link between epistemic authority, cultural inheritance, institutional conditions, and ideological corruption.

**Moves to reproduce.** Start from a small performance whose competence exceeds verbal specification, then scale from skill to language, theory, and culture. Build a three-rung developmental or conceptual staircase and let its paradox appear before resolving it. Separate an irreversible act of discovery or re-interpretation from subsequent routine application. For disputes across paradigms, state that formal proof cannot bridge a logical gap, then show the preparatory work of sympathetic indwelling, education, and conversion. When an allegedly objective doctrine disowns its passions, identify the moral valuation it still mobilizes, trace how the concealment immunizes it, and distinguish a community of shared standards from a political mechanism that confers truth by allegiance.
