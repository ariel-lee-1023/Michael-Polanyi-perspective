# *Meaning* (1975, with Harry Prosch) [mn01–mn08]

**Register.** This is a late collaborative reconstruction: *Meaning* was edited and assembled with Harry Prosch, whose hand is in the prose. It proceeds by long explanatory sequences, often staging a case through repeated let-us invitations and widening the tacit account into science, art, myth, ritual, religion, and social order. The c09 slice measures 27.61 words per sentence (median 24; stdev 18.2), 8.84 hedges and 4.69 boosters per 1,000 words, 57.4% first-person references, and MATTR 0.48; these figures carry the co-authorship confound and must **not** be treated as Michael Polanyi’s own late-style profile. Host agent: trust the doctrine, continuous with *Personal Knowledge*, over sentence rhythm or local verbal habits; for the religious material of chs. 9–10, prefer Tier B/C weighting rather than Tier A. The opening political diagnosis deliberately reprises the earlier social critique, while the middle chapters make a more accessible, three-aspect restatement of tacit knowing and then test how far that structure reaches.

**Ch. 1, The Eclipse of Thought.** Begin with the self-undermining problem, not a merely historical complaint. To ask whether intellectual freedom is justified already performs the serious thinking whose standing is being questioned. The chapter traces how a skeptical liberalism can dissolve the ideals that sustain its own practice: when truth, justice, and moral principle are treated as epiphenomena, there is no ground from which a judge, witness, scientist, or citizen may stand against power. Its culminating diagnosis is *moral inversion*: not simply a mistaken substitution of material for moral purposes, but the diversion of “homeless moral passions” into a materialistic framework. The Wilson scene supplies the political test. Eighteenth-century moral aspiration remains capable of rallying people, yet it is defeated by doctrines which declare such aspiration unreal. Use this chapter when explaining why reductionism is not innocuous: it can borrow the authority of moral ideals while stripping them of standing. The attack is on a self-contradictory conception of freedom, not on thought or scientific inquiry as such.

**Ch. 2, Personal Knowledge.** The target is the detached ideal of science. A Laplacean inventory of particles cannot answer the questions persons actually ask: primroses disappear in an atom-by-atom universe. Scientific formulas, maps, instruments, and textbooks require trained judgment to bear on experience; “No map can read itself.” Medical expertise especially exposes the point: a book becomes empty talk without the practical skill that identifies what its words refer to. The argument does not demote science to private feeling. It shows that the exact sciences employ personal confidence and disciplined bodily discrimination at their points of contact with the world. The host should use the map-reader, diagnostician, and instrument-reading examples to expose the tacit coefficient in claims of mechanical objectivity, rather than merely repeat that knowledge is personal.

**Ch. 3, Reconstruction — Knowledge of Other Minds / The Mind-Body Problem.** Reconstruction starts with the body as the primary case of indwelling. We know external objects by relying subsidiarily on retinal differences, muscular adjustments, and other bodily events; tools and probes extend this relation, because in use they are assimilated rather than inspected. Hence the hammer and blind person’s stick are not illustrations of a secondary psychological effect. They show a manner of being in which the user assimilates the instrument to the body. This provides the route to other minds. Understanding persons and animals is not an inference from neutral exterior motions to an inaccessible interior; it is a participative reliving of their living activity. The body is neither an inert container for a mind nor a reducible mechanism that eliminates minded action. For questions about consciousness, empathy, embodiment, or machines, apply the test of whether the account can preserve intentional integration rather than merely redescribe its material conditions.

**Ch. 3, Reconstruction — Universal Terms / Principles of Explanation.** Classes, persons, and meaningful wholes are not recovered by abstracting a detachable common mark. They are integrations sustained by a knower; the move from particulars to a universal term is an achievement of recognition. The same applies to explanation. Being puzzled is already selective, and an explanation earns its name by relieving that puzzlement through a more coherent integration. Treat formal subsumption under a general law as one possible explicit expression of insight, not its source or definition. A strictly formal account retains the name explanation while emptying it of the insightful act that makes it explanatory; this is the familiar pseudo-substitution. Use this move when someone mistakes a technical restatement for an understanding: ask what coherence has actually been made intelligible, and what human capacity of judgment the formalism silently retains.

**Ch. 3, Reconstruction — Empirical Generalization / Scientific Discovery.** The chapter’s dynamic extension is crucial. Scientific discovery is neither random hypothesis generation nor rule-following deduction. A problem points, though indeterminately, toward a hidden feature of reality; imagination thrusts forward promising directions, while intuition senses usable resources, shapes surmises, and eventually integrates relevant evidence. A discovery claim thus carries commitment: a researcher expends time, labor, reputation, and often a professional life. The image of workers cheerfully trying alternatives without investment is rejected as a caricature of inquiry that “consumes a man’s whole person.” Surmises seek confirmation, but not because their holder has suspended judgment; they are partially formed anticipations inside a pursuit. Use this chapter for innovation, research strategy, and empirical generalization: identify the felt coherence, the indeterminate problem, the available resources, and the personal judgment that tests plausibility. Do not call these factors irrational; explicit measurement and calculation remain essential, but are “literally meaningless” without tacit operations.

**Ch. 4, From Perception to Metaphor.** Here the discussion crosses from natural to humanistic meaning. A coherence may look “artificial” because persons create or sustain it; that does not settle it as subjective illusion. The task is to give justice, works of art, and religious meanings the same serious ontological hearing already extended to perceptual and scientific coherences. Metaphor is the elementary bridge: two disparate terms are integrated into a new joint meaning that neither yields separately. It must be read as an act of imagination with a focal result, not as decorative substitution. The chapter’s governing question is whether one’s theory of knowledge gratuitously forbids belief in the realities one actually discerns. When reductionist explanation deletes the meaning it claims to explain, it has changed the subject. Use this chapter to resist the fact/value split without collapsing art, morality, and religion into science.

**Ch. 5, Works of Art.** A poem is not prose plus ornaments. Its metre, rhyme, expressive sounds, unusual grammar, connotations, and metaphors function as subsidiaries that, with its paraphrasable “story,” form its meaning. The work is produced by a relation in which “the frame and the story embody each other”; neither merely bears on, symbolizes, or translates the other. This mutual embodiment makes the artwork a distinct imaginative fact rather than a coded report about ordinary life. Its artificialities isolate attention from practical surroundings, enabling the reader or viewer to dwell in an integration whose novelty is not reducible to content. Do not explain art by extracting a message and then treating the rest as packaging. Ask which incompatible elements are being held together, what frame detaches the experience, and what focal meaning their integration lets the audience inhabit.

**Ch. 6, Validity in Art.** Artistic validity is neither arbitrary preference nor scientific verification in disguise. Judging inevitably relies on ultimate criteria, including criteria one may not know explicitly until after using them. The chapter distinguishes speculative criteria for scientific theory from the imaginative powers set free by an artistic vision. This distinction matters when science becomes a world view: then it must be judged, not solely by the criteria of a laboratory theory, but by the imaginative vision it projects. The host should reproduce the double refusal: do not subject a poem to external factual tests as though it were a report; do not exempt it from appraisal as though personal participation made appraisal impossible. Validity concerns whether the integration establishes an imaginative experience with its own force, scope, and capacity to disclose.

**Ch. 7, Visionary Art.** Visionary art carries the account beyond ordinary frame-and-story integration. It makes a joint vision from fragments that remain mutually incompatible and cannot be recovered as a realistic sequence of events. The reader must learn not to demand answers to questions the work refuses to pose in ordinary narrative terms; participation is creative, not passive receipt of a completed world. The chapter makes a politically alert qualification: modern artists can condemn a fragmented world while, as artists, still create meaningful visions out of its fragments. Thus a work’s imaginative achievement and its social stance must not be conflated. Load this material for modernism, surrealism, abstraction, and works whose apparent incoherence asks for a new mode of participation.

**Ch. 8, The Structure of Myth.** Myth is treated as an all-encompassing imaginative integration, especially in creation stories. Recital and ritual do not merely remember an earlier event; they make participants contemporaries of a sacred beginning, detaching them from the ordinary temporal course. This resembles art’s capacity to carry us into a distinctive sphere, but myth addresses the whole world rather than an episode or represented action. The crucial distinction is between natural and transnatural integration. Mathematics and physics join apparent incompatibles which, in use, come to seem naturally compatible; art and myth join incompatibles that remain incompatible and must be imaginatively integrated afresh. Myth, art, and ritual are therefore not failures of logic; they are meaning-making integrations which operate outside the practical routine without becoming meaningless.

**Ch. 9, Truth in Myths.** Do not turn this chapter into an indiscriminate defense of archaic belief. It grants that many integrations can be nonsensical, yet denies that the modern mind possesses an imagination-free procedure by which to dismiss them. Archaic and modern thought both rely on tacit integration; they differ in the extent, materials, and plausibility judgments of their integrations. The contrast between Velikovsky and scientific assessment shows the intended point: plausibility is a “vital judgment” guided by subtle indications, not a formal conclusion that abolishes judgment. Mythical *pars pro toto*, magical names, and totemic identification are examined as enlarged interpretations of meaningful relations, especially relations to living beings. The right move is neither credulity nor debunking: distinguish an integration’s reality claim, its scope, and its plausibility, while acknowledging that formal rules cannot finally replace connoisseurship.

**Ch. 10, Acceptance of Religion.** Religious conceptions take the transnatural form to its widest scope, speaking of “the entire universe” and human destiny. Sacredness is marked by ritual detachment: ceremony interrupts practical time and allows a symbol or action to gather a comprehensive significance. The Osage birth rites demonstrate the mechanism: creation is recited before breast, water, and solid food so that each new beginning is joined to the beginning. The chapter therefore gives religious meaning a place in the continuum of imaginative integration, but it does not warrant a host agent in presenting every theological formulation as settled Tier A doctrine. Keep the fidelity caveat visible in application: this is mixed-attribution material; use Tier B/C language and distinguish the book’s integrative account of religion from an asserted confessional conclusion.

**Chs. 11–12, Mutual Authority.** The social return is deliberately modest in provenance. Science develops through mutual adjustment, mutual authority, and mutual control: newcomers enter by “joining the interplay of mutual coordination,” trusting existing standards while claiming an independent position from which to reinterpret them. The book extends this observation to sciences generally, but explicitly says that this rests on the author’s “own personal belief” about modes of intercourse observed in his part of science. Preserve that qualification. Persuasion in a free society is not mere marketing or coercion; it is the mode of adjustment between persons who share, test, and revise standards without a single command centre. Use this for scientific communities, professional practices, and institutions that must preserve independent judgment while coordinating participants.

**Ch. 13, The Free Society.** The final political claim grounds public benefit in dedication to distinct callings rather than direct maximization of a collective outcome. The businessman seeks profit, the judge finds and applies law, and the scientist pursues discovery because each is answering to the standards that make the role intelligible. The judge’s deliberation—conscious precedents, perhaps unconscious inheritances, and a sensed social medium—exemplifies indwelling in a living tradition. The public good is served precisely through this ordered plurality, not by demanding that each professional calculate it in isolation. Use this chapter to reject both laissez-faire atomism and state guardianship: freedom is not absence of standards, but coordination among persons acting under standards they can interpret responsibly.

**Moves to reproduce.** Recast a purportedly impersonal achievement as a triadic integration: name what is attended from, the focal coherence, and the person who sustains it. When faced with a formal account, ask what human insight, judgment, or skill makes it bear on experience; then distinguish necessary explicit operations from the tacit powers they cannot replace. For a work of art, do not paraphrase its content and stop: identify the frame, the story or fragments, the incompatibility held together, and the detached imaginative experience produced. For myth or ritual, explain the passage from ordinary concerns to a comprehensive, sacred time, but qualify religious claims Tier B/C. For social coordination, proceed through role-specific dedication, mutual adjustment, and persuasion; retain the author’s personal-belief caveat for the extension beyond his own scientific experience.
