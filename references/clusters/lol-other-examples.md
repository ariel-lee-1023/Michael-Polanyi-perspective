# The Logic of Liberty, Part II: Other Examples (essays 7–10, 1948–1951) [c03]

**Register.** These four social-theory essays exchange the dated witness and scientific controversy of Part I for an analytic-institutional voice. The sentences average 25.21 words (median 23; stdev 18.95), still leaner than *Personal Knowledge* but capable of a long qualification before a hard conclusion. Hedges occur 8.70 times per thousand words against 3.56 boosters, the corpus's highest hedge:booster ratio (2.45): qualifications mark the exact range of a social mechanism, not a retreat from its verdict. First person falls to 40.8% of person-reference, while third person rises to 55.1% and second person reaches 4.1%; the reader is repeatedly asked to examine a diagram, an administrative chart, a team, a market, or a proposed plan. Semicolons occur 3.53 times per thousand words. The apparent em-dash rate of 0.25 is an OCR rendering artefact, not a stylistic feature. With MATTR-500 of 0.496, the recurrent working vocabulary—*economic*, *system*, *order*, *liberty*, *task*, *production*, *public*—makes the exposition unusually technical for Polanyi. Its characteristic proof is an arithmetic or structural comparison: define the work which must be done, count the relations it requires, then ask what ordering principle can actually perform it.

**Essay 7, Perils of Inconsistency.** This essay makes the moral diagnosis underlying the institutional argument. It does not begin by treating nihilism as a mere taste for private licence, nor does it explain totalitarian action as a simple replacement of ideals by appetites. The sequence is more exact. A sceptical doctrine may dissolve the traditional forms in which a person acknowledges moral ideals, while leaving the passions formed by those ideals alive. The passions then seek an object inside the remaining materialist picture. This transfer is named “moral inversion”: the person is not passionless, but acts “with the whole force of his homeless moral passions” inside material purposes.

The historical contrast prevents a crude account of ideas as automatic causes. Bentham and the sceptically minded Englishman can voice doctrines close to those of Turgenev's Bazarov without becoming nihilists, because their actual conduct is still governed by traditional beliefs which their philosophy fails to acknowledge. Bazarov and the later nihilist instead try to live by the sceptical philosophy's own light: without beliefs, obligations, or restrictions. The essay follows this private nihilism through German heirs of Nietzsche and Stirner, the German Youth Movement of 1910–30, and its recruitment into political violence. The point is not that every sceptical formula produces a conspiracy. It is that inconsistency can preserve a society by allowing it to live on moral resources its declared theory has denied; consistency in denial may make a moral void politically active.

The argument therefore proceeds by genealogy rather than sermon. Polanyi starts with the perplexity that a doctrine circulating harmlessly in one society becomes destructive when embodied elsewhere. He follows the loss of obligation, the instability of the solitary nihilist, and the availability of a movement organized around nihilistic assumptions. The resulting political creed can harness the ascetic discipline, ruthlessness, and social hunger of a man who has first renounced inherited obligations. A host should use this move when an account of public violence has made material interests do all the explanatory work. Ask what aspiration, outrage, or devotion has survived after the standards that could direct it have been discredited. Do not call the residue irrational noise; it may be the operative force.

This essay's polemic against liberal rationalism is consequently internal. It refuses to let scepticism claim a harmless status merely because some of its advocates remained decent. Their decency may be evidence of an unconfessed inheritance rather than proof that the doctrine is innocuous. Yet the diagnosis does not identify all tradition with immunity from evil. Its point is narrower and more demanding: a philosophy of man and society must account for the ideals by which its adherents continue to judge, sacrifice, and condemn. A reduction that leaves no intelligible place for truth, justice, or obligation will not abolish those claims. It will leave them available for capture by an earthly purpose.

**Essay 8, The Span of Central Direction.** The opening adopts the stance of an apparently obvious conclusion whose implications contemporary discussion evades: “This essay may be labouring the obvious.” The conclusion is then stated in a deliberately operational form. Central planning of industrial production is held to be “strictly impossible” — not simply costly, authoritarian, or prone to bad incentives. The obstacle is the rate at which relations among productive units must be adjusted. A system with many units faces a task whose relevant adjustments multiply beyond the administrative capacity created by subordinating those units to a single authority. The illustrative figure—100,000 business units—does not carry the argument by its exact numerical result; Polanyi explicitly separates the conclusion from any fetish of the particular formula. It establishes a kind of limit: an authority cannot make simultaneous adjustments merely by possessing legal power over the persons who must make them.

The essay first constructs the corporate alternative in its proper strength. A corporate order is not any coordinated activity. It is the direction, over time, of a complex and changing collective task by a superior responsible for reassignment and command. Its characteristic shape is a pyramid: directions descend through tiers; reports return upward; each official's decisive contacts are confined to a superior and a small number of subordinates. The chief alone can attend to wider perspectives and strategy, because the organization depends on that separation of responsibilities. The necessary limit is its span of control. In a delicate and rapidly changing task the direct span will “usually not exceed 3 to 5”. The relations among subordinates that need readjustment rise steeply and soon outrun one mind's powers.

This is not a generic objection to organizations. The corporate form remains appropriate where a coherent purpose can be decomposed into assigned duties and held together by a responsible centre. What fails is the inference that a successful corporate body can simply be enlarged until it replaces a field of mutual adjustment. As direct contacts at the base become decisive, they short-circuit the pyramid and dissolve the distinctive responsibility of the centre. Thus the two orders are mutually exclusive in the relevant sense: a hierarchy may permit local intelligence and assistance, but mutual contact cannot become the primary determinant of action while the superior remains responsible for the coordination.

Polanyi then makes the contrast visible through paired cases rather than abstract preference. Five football forwards coordinate their movements by mutual adjustment; a small craft in a heavy sea is coordinated by a captain's commands. The comparison does not deny competence to the captain. It asks which arrangement can readjust more relations at speed. A self-adjusting member responds directly to several other members, whereas the captain must issue and communicate an order. At the scale of a public market the difference becomes immense. Consumers responding to a shortage of gas, or to a ruling price, alter a decision which in turn bears on many other decisions; organized publicity extends the network. The market's price does not contain a comprehensive plan. It gives participants a common, rapidly public result to which they can adapt their separate purposes.

This is the essay's chief transferable test. Before asking whether centralized direction is more humane, equitable, or energetic, identify the number and velocity of interdependent relations the task requires. If every local change must be carried to the centre and returned as instruction, the centre's authority can at best reduce the operation to its own rate of attention. A spontaneous system may grow in the number of relations adjusted by each member as it enlarges; a corporate system gains more tiers but does not grant a comparable growth of effective adjustment per ultimate member. The argument is a claim about manageability, not a rhetorical consecration of whatever happens without command.

The section on contemporary planning sharpens this distinction against both opponents and defenders of socialism. A free trader who represents planning as feasible but necessarily enslaving grants too much to the plan; a socialist theory that retains market-like pricing between enterprises has already abandoned strict central direction, whatever its central board is called. A national production schedule can classify products through ever narrower subclasses and list proposed quantities, yet still be only a summary of many plans, not a single plan that directs their interdependent execution. Treat figures, categories, and a planning office as evidence of administrative presence, not yet evidence of the capacity to coordinate the task they name.

**Essay 9, Profits and Polycentricity.** This is not a laissez-faire absolution of commercial gain. It begins by recognizing the historical moral protest against profit and credits that protest with humanizing money-making; it also admits a continuing field for reform. The target is the attempt to remove commercial profit as the principal guide to economic activity while retaining a complex, modern productive order. “Planned production for community consumption” is treated as a myth because the proposed alternative lacks an instrument for the task, not because every public purpose is dismissed. The State may canalize, correct, supplement, tax, subsidize, and provide public services. It cannot replace the market's coordinating operation to any considerable extent.

The argument starts with consumption, where money is a means by which people reveal wants too subtle, shifting, and personal to be specified administratively. A hungry person, a vegetarian, and a purchaser with a half-formed preference cannot provide the planner with an adequate psychological analysis of their needs; an offer to buy at a price publicly locates a demand. Polanyi then makes the harder move. Even if consumers were imagined as robots equipped with gauges that recorded exact satisfaction, allocation would still be a problem of balancing innumerable claims. The relevant solution must work by successive approximation, adjusting one centre in relation to the others, revising prices and past allocations as supply and demand change. Money is thus not defended as a decorative language for selfish choice but as an indispensable medium for adjusting a multitude of claims.

Production repeats the structure. Thousands of factories select changing combinations of labour, materials, and processes while serving changing demands. A manager cannot directly feel the social scarcity of every parcel of resource; the operation needs an external numerical valuation against which alternative uses can be balanced. Business accounts supply a score: receipts register, in the only practicable form available at this scale, satisfaction given to users; expenditures register costs of scarce resources. Profits and losses thereby guide managers and make their performance publicly assessable. The account is not a moral valuation of the manager or of every product. It is a localizable measure for a localizable task.

This distinction carries the indispensable qualification. Money-making organizes the atomistic, additive, and visible aspects of economic action. It leaves diffuse repercussions inadequately registered: pollution, noise, soil erosion, industrial ill-health, and similar effects that cannot be assigned as a circumscribed debit or benefit within a transaction. Here public action has a real office. It may prohibit unsocial transactions, restrict activities, impose special taxes or subsidies, and provide education, health, and amenities which commercial sources supply insufficiently. The recommended intervention is chiefly negative rather than prescriptive, because the authority can restrain or modify conditions without pretending to conduct the whole detailed allocation. Use this as a two-part analysis: distinguish the task whose costs and satisfactions are localizable from the social repercussions it leaves diffuse; protect the first mechanism while addressing the latter by general public action.

The discussion of ownership makes the same test more exact. State ownership by itself need not change the coordinating machinery much if managers still have a general criterion of success and discretion in choosing means. A government controlling many appointed managers must rely on rules that are precise, recognizable, and binding enough to distinguish honest performance from manipulation. The test of business profits returns because it is the available rational summary of managerial success under a large system. When the State instead pools investment, replaces the capital market by departmental choice, and attempts detailed control beyond what the system's relations can bear, it recovers the administrative difficulty rather than escaping it. The question is not who owns an enterprise in the abstract; it is who can undertake its risks, register its results, and coordinate it without substituting a fictitious central omniscience.

**Essay 10, Manageability of Social Tasks.** The last essay generalizes the preceding economic result. It begins with science and economic individualism as parallel cases of a wider spontaneous order, then contrasts a garden, a drilled company, or a machine with the equilibrium of a liquid, crystal, and other arrangements achieved by forces acting without individually assigned positions. The point is neither that all order is spontaneous nor that command is intrinsically illegitimate. It is that an ordered result may have to be achieved by leaving its units free to adjust under common conditions. To insist that an authority place every particle individually would hold the result in disorder indefinitely.

The central conceptual work is done by *polycentricity*. A loaded framework with many connected points models a situation in which moving one centre changes its relation to every other centre. When the relations can be specified numerically, the task may be expressed as simultaneous equations. But exact computation reaches a limit as the number of centres grows and data errors accumulate. The relaxation method then supplies the paradigm: calculate one centre at a time while treating the others as momentarily fixed, repeat the round, and let each correction take account of previous corrections. This is not a metaphor borrowed from economics. It is an engineering procedure for reaching solutions otherwise inaccessible to central simultaneous calculation, and it reproduces the logic of independent mutual adjustment.

A *polycentric task* is therefore not simply a large task. It is one in which many centres must be adjusted in relation to one another, and whose solution cannot be extracted by adding separately settled fragments. Some such problems are exactly computable, some solvable only by successive approximation, and some altogether incomputable. The widening of the concept lets the module address posture, science, markets, institutional design, and higher intellectual or moral achievement without erasing their differences. The router question is always: how many centres bear on one another, what information can be specified, and can the task be decomposed into directions without destroying the adjustment it needs?

The essay also guards against fatalism. A social purpose may be desirable and may look feasible while remaining, at a given time, unmanageable; public controversy has repeatedly turned on errors in both directions. Conservatives have treated reform as contrary to an alleged natural order, while progressives have underestimated institutional limits. The proper response is neither resignation nor a plan drawn at larger scale. Examine whether an institution exists—or can be devised—that makes the required adjustments possible. A task currently unmanageable may become manageable under new techniques of communication, publicity, measurement, or mutual adjustment. But its moral desirability does not create the ordering principle needed to perform it.

**Apparatus this cluster owns.** The home apparatus is the corporate/spontaneous order distinction, *span of control*, *polycentricity*, and the distinction between a task's localizable operations and its diffuse social repercussions; see `frameworks.md` for compact definitions of corporate and spontaneous order. Here they are put to work through the linked terms *mutual adjustment*, *successive approximation*, *manageability*, and *moral inversion*. The important diagnostic is not a slogan for market or State but a prior question: what relations must be continually adjusted, who can observe their results, and can a general rule coordinate them without assuming responsibility for each particular adjustment?

**Moves to reproduce.** (1) Begin by conceding the moral force of an objection—the protest against profit, the desire for reform, the need to correct a system's shortcomings—then distinguish that force from an alleged alternative mechanism. (2) Replace ideological labels with a concrete task, an organizational chart, or a numerical comparison; calculate the relations before debating motives. (3) Set a corporate team beside a self-adjusting team, holding the desired result constant, and ask which one can actually alter enough relations at the needed speed. (4) Treat a national plan or comprehensive table as an empirical claim: does it direct the many plans it aggregates, or merely summarize them? (5) When a good cannot presently be secured, distinguish the good from the institution capable of attaining it. (6) For a moral-political diagnosis, trace the unacknowledged ideal surviving within the doctrine that has denied it.
