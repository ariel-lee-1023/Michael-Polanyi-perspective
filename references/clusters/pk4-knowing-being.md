# Personal Knowledge, Part Four: Knowing and Being (chs. 11–13) [c07]

**Register.** This final Part changes the book’s object from the personal coefficient in science to reality disclosed in machines, organisms, persons, and human history as achievements. It is still architectonic exposition, but more openly affirmative and climactic, with the argument repeatedly enlarged from a technical case to ontology and then to moral obligation. Its 26.63-word mean sentence (median 25; stdev 14.30) sits near the corpus baseline, while first-person procedure is unusually high (64.8% of person references) and third-person reference low (33.7%); the voice keeps saying what it will establish and what has been shown. Hedges are low for *Personal Knowledge* (7.29/1k) and boosters relatively high (4.36/1k), semicolons 3.76/1k and em dashes 4.62/1k. Its lexical field is biological and technical—*knowledge, animal, living, terms, process, principles, machine, system*—while *people, idea, social,* and *political* are diagnostic absences. The chapter sequence therefore makes a rising argument rather than a social programme: contrivance, life, then anthropogenesis.

**Ch. 11, The Logic of Achievement — make function prior to inventory.** The opening redirection is from the logic of deduction and empirical inference to “the logic of contriving.” The latter had reached philosophy only in “scattered hints,” Polanyi says, because attempts to render knowledge strictly impersonal had missed a kind of knowledge that cannot be impersonal. The basic contrast is sharp: an observational statement may record a discovery; an operational principle specifies a contrivance and, if novel, can be patented. A clock, typewriter, boat, telephone, locomotive, or camera is not gathered into its relevant class by shared material or shape. Its parts count as organs only in their coordinated contribution to an overall operation and purpose. This is why a patent strives to formulate the widest possible operational principle while avoiding accidental physical or chemical particulars of a particular construction.

The chapter makes the operational principle normative without making it subjective. “Technology comprises all acknowledged operational principles and endorses the purposes which they serve”; it also appraises a machine as a rational means to its acknowledged advantage. The principle functions as an ideal—“the ideal of the machine in good working order”—and establishes a standard by which an item is a more or less perfect clock, typewriter, or locomotive. It thereby introduces distinctions that a neutral description cannot supply: orderly performance and malfunction, reason and cause, success and failure. A boiler may burst and a crankshaft snap, but these are failures only against the antecedent rule defining what the machine ought to do. The analogy to geometrical crystallography is deliberate: the idealized conception ignores the particular imperfections of failed instances in order to specify right functioning.

Physics and chemistry can explain a strain, corrosion, or a break; they do not thereby supersede technical knowledge. A complete account of a clock’s configurations, even with all future configurations predicted, would not say that it is a clock. The compressed maxim is that complete physical description does not identify a machine as a machine. The point is not hostility to physical explanation but an ordering of questions. Technical understanding identifies an object through participation in its purpose and endorsement of its operational principle; physical or chemical inquiry enters only as subsidiary knowledge bearing on the conditions of that functioning or of its breakdown. A comprehensive feature lets the knower recognize the object, while lower-level description remains blind to the feature by which recognition was made.

This allows the chapter to distinguish causes from reasons. A technological procedure is a rational stratagem: its stages and parts have specifiable reasons within a coherent operation aimed at an acknowledged end. Causes may explain why a failure occurs; reasons explain why a part belongs in the procedure at all. Do not flatten the contrast into the claim that causal explanation is false. The move is to show that causal description does not contain the vocabulary in which purposive success was first identified.

**Ch. 11 — machine, regulation, and the personal fact.** The progression begins with animal capacities to perform tricks, read signs, and find their way, treated as primordial forms of contriving, observing, and reasoning. It then extends through engineering, natural science, and mathematics, and finally asks what happens when the achievement is living. Physiology is named “the technology of healthy achievements”: feeding, digestion, locomotion, perception, and reproduction are intelligible as performances serving an organism. Yet an organism is not merely a machine. Living beings function by two “always interwoven principles,” as machines and by regulation. Fixed structures yield the machine-like case; regulation is an equipotential integration in which parts may reorganize their contribution to the joint performance. The former can be stated with relatively precise operational principles; the latter is appraised only in gestalt-like terms and calls for connoisseurship.

The distinction should not be used to erase the common ground. Both modes are achievements of a comprehensive biotic entity; both are known under rules of rightness; both are unavailable to the impersonal terms of physics and chemistry alone. The chapter’s examples—chimpanzees reorganizing a visual field, rats adapting a learned maze after injury, earthworms responding resourcefully to altered conditions, and a person’s unformulated adjustment of muscular coordination—place originality already on the animal side of the human–animal continuum. The point is that resourceful integration outruns a catalogue of isolated components.

From this follows the vocabulary of *personal fact*. “To the extent to which our personal participation in knowing a fact contributes to making it what it is,” the fact may be called personal; where this knowing is unspecifiable, the object cannot be exhausted by less personal particulars. This becomes decisive when the knower recognizes an individual. An individual organism has a centre: it grows, produces meaningful acts, and sustains itself. The knower’s relation is correspondingly transformed. Sign-learning at first places an observer before an object; in recognizing centres of activity and eventually other persons, “The I-It situation has been gradually transformed into an I-Thou relation.” The ascent from fact toward moral and civic affirmation is thus prepared inside biological knowing, not appended as an unrelated ethical doctrine.

**Ch. 12, Knowing Life — begin with the organism’s achievement.** This chapter makes biology an appraisal rather than a mere inventory. “living things are more highly personal” than facts of the inanimate world, because higher life demands ever more far-reaching participation by the knower. The logical gap between comprehension and its specification deepens up the evolutionary ladder; at the same time, the subject matter increasingly includes the very faculties by which it is understood. The task is to notice that morphology, physiology, psychology, and ethology already select their objects under questions of normality, achievement, error, competence, and development.

The diagnostic form is practical: before analysing an organ, first ask what it is for and how it works. “The study of an organ must begin with an attempt to guess what it is for and how it works.” Physiological and physico-chemical inquiries then proceed as coordinated inquiries within that purposive framework. To undertake the latter irrespective of physiological assumptions is, as a rule, to generate meaningless results; to replace physiology by a physical-chemical chart is to dissolve understanding of the organism. Valves are explained as preventing regurgitation because the organism’s interest supplies the relevant reason; the causes of an actual regurgitation may then be sought. Physiology is teleological in this restricted and operational sense.

The same logic governs morphology. A physical-chemical explanation is indifferent to successful or failed gestalt seeing, and therefore cannot represent the distinction between illusion and knowledge. Morphogenesis forms right shapes and may succeed or fail; a lower-level account can at most shift the question back to the rightness of the starting conditions. Normal and abnormal forms must be recognized before mathematical or physical analysis can refer to them. The deliberately severe formulation is that a complete physical and chemical topography of a frog tells nothing about it as a frog unless it is already known as a frog. This is a reduction test: ask whether the base description can state the prior biological shape or function which makes the data relevant.

The chapter extends this test to psychology and learning. Psychology differs from astronomy because it studies achievements believed to be mental, not events divorced from purpose. A mechanism of learning presupposes recognizable situations, correct responses, and a standard of acquired knowledge. Behavioural regularities are consequently not self-interpreting proofs that the achievement has disappeared. The criticism of logical behaviourism presses precisely here: a theory which claims to eliminate mentality must still use the appraisals by which learning, recognition, and error are identified.

The conclusion reconnects biology with the fiduciary argument of Part Three. The biological hierarchy initially seems three-levelled: the organism, the inquirer, and the superior standards underwriting inquiry. When attention extends to “active beings centred on themselves,” that arrangement becomes a two-levelled commitment in which man stands below the ideals he serves—the personal pole beneath the universal pole. Biology meets philosophical self-accrediting at this point of confluence. Knowing life becomes the continuous exercise of the same responsibility that, at its highest, acknowledges truth and human greatness.

**Ch. 13, The Rise of Man — re-open the world as an ascending achievement.** The final chapter refuses to conclude with a closed natural theory. Its aim is instead “to re-equip men with the faculties which centuries of critical thought have taught them to distrust.” The results are restated in a sequence: living beings are known in terms of success and failure; a successful system is known as a whole while its particulars are held subsidiarily; and ascending levels of achievement cannot be represented exhaustively by lower-level particulars. Evolution then becomes the hard case: how can a stratified world have emerged continuously from material elements without either reducing its higher forms to those elements or inserting an unrelated creative agent at every stage?

The answer uses the logic of achievement to recover emergence. The heuristic leap across a logical gap provides the paradigm: a discovery is not derived by a rule from the clues that guide it, yet it is not arbitrary. Likewise, the evolutionary story must distinguish the conditions that release and sustain an achievement from the principle by which a new comprehensive order operates. Random molecular agitation and environmental circumstances may release or sustain a process; they are not, merely by being conditions, the order of innovation. The chapter therefore calls for an “orderly innovating principle” and sees direct evidence for it in the emergence from submicroscopic living matter of “sentient, responsible and creative beings.”

Its challenge to mechanistic accounts should be stated in its own terms. An acid does not dissolve platinum “by mistake”; a chemical action has no standard it can satisfy or fail. An insentient automaton therefore cannot represent a responsible person simply by reproducing regular sequences. The thought–neural-process distinction is not a duplication of vocabulary for one object but a distinction of subject matter: thoughts belong to thinkers; acids do not think. Nor does reference to quantum mechanics solve the issue. Quantum calculation may predict a distribution under any stated conditions, but it cannot state which conditions ought to be given for a chemical inquiry; those are fixed by practical skill and the interests of chemists. The chapter’s contention is thus logical and epistemic before it is empirical: lower-level laws leave open higher-level questions, norms, and operations.

Anthropogenesis gives the argument its ethical pitch. The ascent through individual life, sentience, personhood, and culture is narrated as an intensification of centres that take the hazards of living and believing. The *noosphere* names the culminating firmament of superior knowledge, but the conclusion refuses a merely zoological ranking. Man is named the “most precious fruit of creation” and this knowledge lies outside natural science because it is possessed through submission to a firmament of obligations believed equally binding on all human beings. The study of man’s rise consequently reaches beyond biology to what is believed about human nature and destiny. This is not a detachable devotional coda: it is the final consequence of a mode of knowing that always already judges achievement under standards it cannot reduce to impersonal inventory.

An operational principle groups materially diverse constructions by their joint functioning toward an acknowledged purpose, while a rule of rightness identifies the ideal performance against which success and malfunction appear. **Moves to reproduce.** Begin by identifying an achievement that a lower-level description has silently presupposed: a clock’s working, an organ’s function, a frog’s recognizable form, a learned response, a responsible decision. Then ask the prior question: what counts here as success, failure, normality, error, or purpose? Distinguish reasons internal to an acknowledged operation from causes of its material conditions or breakdown, without denying the latter. Make the opponent’s strongest data subsidiary rather than irrelevant: physical and chemical facts matter when they bear on the operational principle; they cannot supply that principle from within themselves. Build the argument by ascending cases—machine, organism, sentient being, person, culture—so that the final normative claim is shown as continuous with, rather than pasted onto, a description of life. In evolutionary questions, separate a random variation or favourable environment that releases an event from the ordering innovation it cannot itself specify. Finish by making obligation explicit: the question is not only what has occurred, but under which standards the achievement was identifiable as such.
