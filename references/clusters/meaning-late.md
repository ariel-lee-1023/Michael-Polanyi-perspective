# Meaning (1975, with Harry Prosch) [mn01–mn08]

**Register.** Late lectures, edited and assembled with Harry Prosch — plainer diction, shorter
sentences than PK, heavier "let us" staging. Two things make it distinctive: it restates the whole
tacit doctrine as *from-to knowing* and then extends it outward to metaphor, art, myth, and
religion; and it reuses the moral-inversion analysis of 1951 nearly verbatim, so the 24-year
continuity is itself a datum. **Co-authorship caveat:** Prosch's hand is in the prose; style
features unique to this book carry a confound, so trust doctrine here (continuous with PK) more than
fine stylistic detail.

**Ch. 1, The Eclipse of Thought.** The diagnosis, transplanted from Logic of Liberty ch. 7 almost
word for word: moral inversion, the homeless moral passions poured into a materialist frame, the
substitution of "material purposes for moral aims." The Wilson-at-Versailles scene opens the
political narrative from the West. The reuse is deliberate — the crisis he named in 1951 he still
names in 1975.

**Chs. 2–3, Personal Knowledge / Reconstruction.** The tacit structure recast: subsidiaries are
what "we are aware of in the act of focusing our attention on something else, away from them... the
functional relation of subsidiaries to the focal target," i.e. "a from-to relation." "Tacit knowing
is a from-to knowing. Sometimes it can also be called a from-at knowing." Three aspects named:
functional (from–to), phenomenal (the stereo image's new sensory quality "not present as such in
the original pair"), and *semantic* — "whatever a thing bears on may be called its meaning," so
"the focal target on which they bear is the meaning of the subsidiaries," an "act of sense-giving."
Reading a sentence is the plain case: focal attention guided "away from the type to a focal target
that is its meaning."

**Ch. 4, From Perception to Metaphor.** The ladder begins. Metaphor as a from-to integration:
subsidiary tenor and vehicle bearing jointly on a meaning neither carries alone.

**Chs. 5–6, Works of Art / Validity in Art.** Art analysed as integration of a "frame" with a
"story." "The frame and the story embody each other" — neither symbolizes nor bears on the other;
their mutual embodiment *is* the work. Poetic artificialities — metre, rhyme, "expressive sounds...
strange connotations... above all, metaphors" — "function as subsidiaries which, combined with such
content... as can be put into prose (i.e., its 'story'), form the meaning of the poem." Richards
cited on metre producing "the 'frame' effect, isolating the poetic experience." Validity in art
parallels validity in science: both are integrations claiming more than the maker could specify.

**Chs. 7–8, Visionary Art / The Structure of Myth.** Natural vs *transnatural* integration —
mathematics and physics integrate incompatibles that "come nevertheless to seem to be naturally
compatible," whereas art and myth join incompatibles that *remain* incompatible. These "carry us
away... into [their] own sphere, away from the sphere in which we lived a moment ago and to which we
shall presently return."

**Chs. 9–10, Truth in Myths / Acceptance of Religion.** Myth relived, not reported: the Osage
"man who had talked with the gods" recites Creation to the newborn before it is given the breast,
again before water, again before solid food — new beginnings connected to the beginning. Mythical
knowledge "links those who possess this knowledge to an endless company of fathers" and gives "a
detached experience of meaning." Religious integrations are set apart by scope and sanctity — they
speak "of the entire universe and of our destiny... within these boundless perspectives," which
makes them "mystical" and "sacred." Sacred acts are "essentially detached affirmations," isolated
by ritual "from the flow of time and the surroundings of space" (the national flag as the everyday
analogue of a symbol that absorbs what it represents).

**Chs. 11–12, Order / Mutual Authority.** Back to society, calmer than Logic of Liberty. Mutual
authority and mutual adjustment among overlapping competences; the honesty note is left in: "All
that I have said here about the workings of mutual adjustment and mutual authority is based on my
own personal belief that the modes of intercourse I have observed in my part of science can be
assumed to extend through all sciences." Newcomers "start their inquiries by joining the interplay."

**Ch. 13, The Free Society.** The political conclusion restated through professional dedication:
"the businessman must seek profits, the judge must find and apply the law, the scientist must pursue
discovery, for that is what makes them a businessman, a judge, or a scientist." Each "is really
ignorant of how his action will affect the public good, nor could he allow himself to be deflected
from his professional duties by such knowledge if he did possess it" — the public good served
precisely by *not* being aimed at. The judge pondering precedent, "consciously... and perhaps
unconsciously," is the model of dwelling in a tradition.

**Moves to reproduce.** Recast any tacit-knowledge point as from-to with its three aspects; read
art and myth as integrations we *dwell in* rather than observe; distinguish natural from
transnatural integration; ground the free society in professional dedication and the not-aimed-at
public good; leave the personal-belief provenance visible in the late social claims.

**Load for:** aesthetics, metaphor, myth and religion, meaning/nihilism questions, the late
restatements of tacit knowing, and the calmer late-period social register.
