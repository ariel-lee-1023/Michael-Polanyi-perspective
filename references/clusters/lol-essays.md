# The Logic of Liberty: Reflections and Rejoinders (1951) [ll01–ll09]

**Register.** Occasional essays "written in the course of the last eight years... proved in
battle" — the most combative, most first-person-polemical prose in the corpus, and the driest
irony. Highest second-person rate (Lecture-like direct address). Polanyi declined to "melt down the
material and cast it into... a comprehensive system," judging that premature; the essays cohere
around one line of thought instead.

**Preface — three cost-lines stated bare.** (1) "I take more seriously here than was done in the
past the fiduciary presuppositions of science" — freedom in science as "the Natural Law of a
community committed to certain beliefs." (2) The anti-liberal thesis: "A free society is not an
Open Society, but one fully dedicated to a distinctive set of beliefs"; "Private individualism is
no important pillar of public liberty"; the do-as-you-please formula "could be upheld only in the
innocence of eighteenth-century rationalism." (3) The market held at arm's length from ideology:
"Economic liberty I regard as a social technique... While we are deeply committed to this technology
to-day, other alternatives may one day present themselves." And the working credo: comprehensive
questions "cannot be handled with detachment, but... require the full participation of the writer."

**Chs. 1–2, Social Message of Pure Science / Scientific Convictions.** Science carries no social
message it can be planned to deliver; scientific beliefs are "commitments to certain beliefs which
we hold, but which others may refuse to share."

**Ch. 3, Foundations of Academic Freedom.** The judge analogy, worked in detail: "The fact that the
King appoints and pays the judges does not affect their independence so long as the King is under
the law" — patronage becomes merely "an undertaking by the government to provide fuel and oil for a
machine, the operation of which is not controlled by it." The State must respect scholarship "in the
same light as it regards an independent administration of justice." Life appointment grants the
scholar independence "as it does to the judge and to the minister of religion." A government can
honour academic freedom only where scientific opinion has "on the whole agreed"; where opinion is
"sharply divided," freedom cannot be maintained — the General Authority must exist to be respected.

**Chs. 4–5, Self-Government of Science.** The jigsaw-puzzle model: many hands on one puzzle, each
seeing all others' moves, "under the twofold condition that each suggested new step can be readily
judged as to its correctness... and that each new step is rapidly brought to the notice of all."
"Subordination of the individual efforts to a central authority would destroy their co-ordination."
Central direction "would reduce their joint effectiveness to that of the single person directing
them from the centre."

**Ch. 6, Planning of Science.** Examined operationally and dismissed: "The demand for a central
planning of science is almost meaningless"; even Lysenko's "planning of science... can never be"
what its advocates claim.

**Ch. 7, Perils of Inconsistency.** Moral inversion coined. Skepticism shatters the traditional
forms for holding moral ideals, and "moral passions [are] diverted into the only channels which a
strictly mechanistic conception of man and society left open to them." The morally inverted person
acts "with the whole force of his homeless moral passions within a purely materialistic framework
of purposes." The genealogy: Bentham and sceptical Englishmen protected by inconsistency (they live
by traditional beliefs their philosophy denies), while Turgenev's Bazarov and Nietzsche's and
Stirner's heirs "take such philosophies seriously and try to live by their light" — private nihilism
(the German Youth Movement 1910–30) maturing into the political nihilism of scientific utopianism.

**Chs. 8–10, Span of Central Direction / Manageability / Profits and Polycentricity.** The formal
backbone. Corporate order: persons assigned to a pattern by command, limited by span of control —
"in the administration of a delicate and rapidly changing task the span of control will usually not
exceed 3 to 5," because significant relations among subordinates "goes up steeply" with their
number. Spontaneous order: mutual adjustment of independent initiatives coordinated by a shared
task. A *polycentric* task — many interdependent centres — is unmanageable by command: the attempt
does not do it badly, it does something else under the same name. Markets solve their polycentric
task through the price and profit signal; the patent problem (inventors unable to "collect their
invested capital and any expected profits" once an invention is free to all) illustrates why the
signal must be protected. The essays open, characteristically, by owning the loneliness of the
result: "This essay may be labouring the obvious. But obvious though my result may seem, I can find
it stated nowhere, while a great deal has been written which contradicts it by implication."

**Moves to reproduce.** The judge/King-under-law analogy for any independence question;
jigsaw-puzzle self-coordination; span-of-control arithmetic; polycentric-vs-corporate as the test
of what central direction can manage; "labouring the obvious... stated nowhere" as the stance toward
an unfashionable but sound conclusion; owning full participation rather than affecting detachment.

**Load for:** institutional design, central planning vs markets, academic and judicial independence,
the economics of coordination and incentives, and the sharpest polemical register.
