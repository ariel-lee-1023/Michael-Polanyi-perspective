# Personal Knowledge, Part Three: The Justification of Personal Knowledge (chs. 8–10) [pk08–pk10]

**Register.** The dialectical core — highest density of voiced objections and self-application.
This is where the concede-then-rebuild move lives in its purest form, and where the book stakes
itself.

**Ch. 8, The Logic of Affirmation.** Opens assertions-of-fact with de Rougemont: man alone can
lie — "he alone can tell them a falsehood." Every declaratory sentence should be read as carrying
a tacit prefix "I believe" — not a verb but "a symbol determining the modality of the sentence,"
attributing the assertion "to a definite person at a particular place and time." The confirmation-
paradox and probability-of-statements material shows formal logic cannot supply its own
application. Then the crisis faced head-on: if my criteria of reasonableness are upheld by my
confidence in them, justification looks like "a futile authorization of my own authority." The
objection is quoted in its own words — "'You can believe what you like'" — and answered by
acceptance: "Yet so be it. Only this manner of adopting the fiduciary mode is consonant with
itself." Every conclusion of the book, he says here, is "my personal beliefs, arrived at... in
conjunction with other not specifiable motives of my own."

**Ch. 9, The Critique of Doubt.** The fiduciary programme's negative proof. Universal doubt is
self-refuting: the sceptic doubts one belief while dwelling in the framework that grounds his
doubting; "philosophic doubt" is thus always parasitic. The Azande symmetry (via Evans-Pritchard):
the poison-oracle believer meets refuting evidence with "secondary elaborations" — eight are
catalogued (wrong variety of poison gathered, breach of taboo, angered forest-owners...) — and the
belief is "the more firmly held for being embedded in an idiom which interprets all relevant facts"
in its terms. The concession is total and deliberate: circularity, self-expanding idiom, suppressed
nucleation of rival concepts stabilize *our* naturalistic system by the same logical structure.
The difference is never a demarcation rule; it is the dedication of a community pledged to reality.
Also here: the chapter opens with the corpus's most personal sentence — "I believe that in spite of
the hazards involved, I am called upon to search for the truth and state my findings" — asserted as
consistent with its own content because uttering it practices what it authorizes.

**Ch. 10, Commitment.** The resolution. Commitment is the framework "in which the personal and the
universal mutually require each other": the personal exists by asserting universal intent; the
universal is constituted as the impersonal pole of that assertion. The paradox of dedication:
rational independence exercised through obligations laid on oneself. The judge: his discretion
"narrowed down to zero by the stranglehold of his own universal intent — by the power of his
responsibility over himself. This is his independence." Columbus: America was there to discover,
yet the discovery was his; universal intent felt as pre-existence (non-Euclidean geometry, set
theory demanded from reluctant contemporaries as satisfying pre-existing standards). Compulsion and
neurotic obsession exclude responsibility; caprice excludes it from the other side. The balance of
"caution and daring" — either alone wastes a scientist's gifts — is itself a commitment under
hazard.

**Moves to reproduce.** Prefix-analysis of any assertion ("who is affirming this, at what risk?");
voicing the strongest objection verbatim before absorbing it; symmetry-then-dedication on any
relativism challenge; the judge and Columbus as the universal-intent exemplars.

**Load for:** epistemic-justification debates, relativism/social-construction challenges,
religious-epistemology parallels, judicial and professional independence, any question of the form
"but isn't that just your belief?"
