# Science, Faith and Society (1946; “Background and Prospect” added 1964) [sfs01–sfs04]

**Register.** These Riddell Memorial Lectures are the compact, public origin statement: an address to hearers who must be shown why science is free without pretending that it is self-grounding. The c01 slice averages 25.2 words per sentence (median 23; stdev 14.98), shorter than the later *Personal Knowledge* exposition; it has 8.69 hedges and 5.25 boosters per 1,000 words, a 1.66 hedge:booster ratio, 3.44 semicolons and 4.12 em dashes per 1,000, and a third-person-heavy person reference pattern (44.9% first, 1.5% second, 53.6% third). Its MATTR is 0.523. The lecturing voice asks a plain question, runs a concrete scientific or historical case, and then gives the principle a capitalized name. *Premisses* is the recurrent working word. The 1964 “Background and Prospect” changes the footing from lecture to dated witness-statement: August 1938, Easter 1935, December 1945, Vavilov, Lysenko, Bukharin. Use this cluster when the argument must be both an account of inquiry and a public confession of the grounds on which its freedom is defended.

**“Background and Prospect” (1964).** This retrospective is not prefatory scenery. It identifies the political experience that forced the philosophical question. In August 1938 the British Association founded a Division meant to give social guidance to science; a movement for planning accordingly became predominant among scientists concerned with public affairs. Polanyi places himself among the small opposing minority. When the Division asked him to open its December 1945 discussion, he renewed the case for the traditional independence of inquiry and expected hostility, only to find the meeting in favour of science pursued for its own sake. The dated sequence lets him write as a participant whose view was tested in a controversy, rather than as a theorist retrospectively decorating a doctrine.

The Moscow scene supplies the sharper test. Bukharin explained that the pure/applied distinction was a capitalist illusion: Soviet scientists would remain free, but the harmony of socialist society would lead them to work that served the current Five Year Plan. The alleged reconciliation of untrammelled inquiry with a prescribed social end appears here as a contradiction with consequences. Vavilov’s persecution by Lysenko then makes visible what ideological authority can do to biology. Yet the decisive difficulty is not merely that Soviet policy was brutal. When Vavilov invoked Western science against Lysenko, he too appealed to an authority. The question pressed upon Polanyi is therefore: on what grounds can a scientific community accredit its own authority? His answer reaches the personal limit without evasive impersonalism: “At some point I can only answer, ‘For I believe so’.”

The retrospective also supplies the social extension of the lectures. Thought needs legal and economic protection, and profit and power will interact with it; but science cannot be bent from its intrinsic interests, only stunted or stopped by an infringement of autonomy. A community of discovery must guarantee independent action in the service of jointly upheld values. The 1964 wording revises the earlier “spiritual reality” toward the “reality of emergent meaning and truth,” but it preserves the functional claim: creative communal life rests on confidence that hidden truth may still be revealed. Load this section for a response that must turn a political controversy into the question of what an autonomous intellectual tradition presupposes.

**Lecture I, “Science and Reality.”** Begin against the image of science as an automatic conversion of measurements into law. Given a table of figures, indefinitely many mathematical functions can fit it; a rule cannot select the natural law. Nor is a scientific proposition simply a forecast of instrument readings. The burglar heard in an adjoining room provides the better case. To conclude that there is a burglar is to assert a real entity that may show itself through indefinitely various and still unforeseen observations. Hence a theory can bear distant, unexpected fruit without having been mechanically derivable from an initial list of data. The scientist does not merely arrange signs; he seeks a coherent hidden reality.

The observation/discovery relation is stated in a reversible sequence: “The part of observation is to supply clues for the apprehension of reality”; the reality thus apprehended becomes a clue to later observations. This does not license arbitrary conjecture. It identifies the work which rules alone cannot do. Discovery and verification both involve “an intuition of the relation between observation and reality”; verification is usually more rule-governed, yet in the last resort it too exceeds “the application of any definite rules.” Use the distinction to refuse both caricatures at once: the scientist is neither a passive collector of facts nor a sovereign inventor whose preference makes a result true.

Polanyi reinforces the point through the history of resistance to inconvenient data. The periodic system and Einstein’s quantum theory of light were not simply discarded when observations conflicted with them. An anomaly can reveal a limitation, an auxiliary error, or a deeper meaning; laboratory work would become a chase after imaginary novelties if every odd result were treated as a fundamental refutation. No rule decides in advance whether an exception destroys a proposition or carries it farther. This is why the material terms of science include judgement, responsibility, and a readiness to carry a promising conception through its hazards.

The name for the guiding background is the *premisses of science*: “the fundamental guesses of science concerning the nature of things.” They determine which questions look reasonable and interesting, which conceptions are kept alive despite counter-evidence, and which apparent confirmations are rejected as unlikely. Established propositions enter these premisses and affect what counts as an observation fit to be accepted as fact. They are continuously modified, not immune from criticism; the advances of science are described as the discernment of Gestalten that disclose aspects of reality. This is an early, concentrated account of the tacit coefficient in science, but it should be used here in the language of guesses, clues, perception, and premisses rather than imported later terminology.

The lecture gives discovery an experiential rhythm. A researcher spends months in fruitless trials, sustained by a fascination that survives repeated defeat; a vague shape of truth sharpens, dissolves under further thought, and sometimes gains strength through both reflection and evidence. A claim becomes public when the investigator accepts responsibility for printing it. Scientific propositions therefore “appear to be in the nature of guesses.” The immediate brace is essential: “As I am convinced that there is great truth in science I do not consider its guesses as unfounded.” Reproduce the two halves together. This cluster never treats fallibility as an excuse for sceptical withdrawal.

A further passage distinguishes research from manufacture. Rules of art can help, but applying a precise prescription produces an object, whereas acquiring new knowledge by prescribed manipulation produces a survey, not a discovery. The scientist’s major function is to find a good problem, choose the surmise, and recognize the solution. The language can become notably visionary: potential discovery attracts the mind that will reveal it, leading the trained eye and ransacked brain from clue to clue. That manner belongs to c01’s open conjunction of science and faith; use it for the calling and discipline of discovery, not as a generic claim that every innovation is good.

**Lecture II, “Authority and Conscience.”** The second lecture asks how a practice which needs independent judgement can transmit itself. An art cannot be exactly defined and must be learned from examples embodied in practice. The novice must trust a master’s example, recognize the authority of the art, and presume the soundness of its substance and method before acquiring a sense of scientific value. The inherited phrase *fides quaerens intellectum*—“to believe in order to know”—marks the order of initiation. Imitation does not impose permanent submission: it trains the novice to stand on the same ground and eventually claim independence upon it.

This produces a distinctive account of tradition. The scientific tradition is not a storehouse of settled propositions. It teaches the pupil to value originality, including originality that opposes current teaching; in transmitting itself it “should invite opposition to itself” and later assimilate that opposition in a reinterpretation of the tradition. A pioneer confronting scientific opinion as it is appeals to scientific opinion as it ought to be, and appeals to Pasteur, Semmelweis, Lister, Arrhenius, and van ’t Hoff as earlier instances. Thus a radical claim is tested neither by mere deference nor by naked rupture. It seeks a common ground with its opponents and offers itself as the more faithful realization of the tradition’s own standards.

The master–pupil relation exposes the risks on both sides. A student who labels a bungled analysis a discovery must be corrected; a master who pressures students to confirm the master’s theory must be opposed more firmly. The difference is not between regulation and freedom, but between conscientious judgment and coercive substitution for it. These conflicts ordinarily remain workable because master and pupil share “a sufficiently authentic vision” of science to recognize common ground. Use the pair for questions about mentorship, research governance, peer review, or education: the standard is a living art that authorizes dissent while excluding fraud, pretence, and incapacity.

Scientific conscience is the ultimate interpreter of rules, not mere punctiliousness in obeying them. It arbitrates between intuitive impulse and critical procedure, and scientific opinion organizes it through a shared cultivation of ideals. The authority of science resides in scientific opinion: its unofficial self-government is focused on particular occasions by competent experts commanding broad confidence. Each scientist is criticized by neighbours who are in turn criticized by theirs; the chain of mutual appraisal preserves standards across an immense division of labour. The “Republic of Science” is consequently an order in which every position is sovereign in the relevant act of judgement, though influence and competence are uneven.

Do not reduce this to a vote, a professional cartel, or an early version of a managerial network. Consensus is valuable only because it can resolve the permanent tension between discipline and originality. Scientific administration should protect the conditions of spontaneous initiative—appointments, grants, journals, and standards—rather than direct mature researchers to predetermined conclusions. A central decider of every scientific question would render most decisions valueless, halt progress, and repel those who love discovery. The lecture thereby supplies the institutional texture behind the more formal authority distinction of Lecture III.

**Lecture III, “Dedication or Servitude.”** Here the argument turns from the internal life of science to freedom in society. Sovereignty in science is divided into fragments, each exercised by a scientist when conscience and personal belief must decide a question. Polanyi contrasts two modes of authority. One lays down general presuppositions, entrusting their interpretation to many independent persons and relying on their consciences; the other centralizes decisions at headquarters. “We may call the first a General, the latter a Specific Authority.” The distinction diagnoses an institution by the site of interpretation, not merely by whether it has rules, public funds, offices, or sanctions.

General Authority is indispensable in science because a common tradition gives independent judgements a shared direction. Specific Authority imposed on the content of inquiry is destructive for the same reason. The lecture extends the form to law and Protestant religion: bodies of opinion, rooted in a tradition, maintain a measure of self-interpretation even where formal compulsion exists. The proper political demand is not freedom in the abstract. A scientist’s membership entails dedication to a spiritual reality and therefore an obligation to act according to conscience. “Freedom of this kind” is “freedom to act according to particular obligations.” This cluster’s liberty is dedication rather than personal licence.

The comparison of rival overarching positions completes the movement from science to faith. Experience cannot mechanically decide between different total premisses; as common ground diminishes, argument becomes an attempted conversion. Each party must expose the poverty of the other position and induce a glimpse of a richer perspective, trusting that a new intellectual and spiritual satisfaction can draw the other onward. The judgement is akin to discovery: intuition and conscience, disciplined by a search for reality, rather than a conclusive deduction from neutral data. This is a move to reproduce when a disagreement concerns the grounds on which evidence, standards, and meaning are held at all.

The lecture then refuses the self-cancelling denial of truth. “It is logically false to deny the existence of truth” because the assertion of that denial assumes truth can be established. Equally, the formula that truth is whatever benefits a class does not abolish transcendence; it transfers the obligations owed to truth to a temporal interest. Materialism and romanticism can deprive citizens of grounds for appealing to truth, justice, and morality, while total power claims to embody them. This is the earliest home for the argument later named moral inversion. Use the exact later coinage sparingly here; c01 gives its political and spiritual anatomy, while c03 owns the sustained genealogy.

**Moves to reproduce.** Open a science question by denying neither science nor fallibility: identify the hidden reality to which evidence is a clue, then show why a rule cannot itself select or interpret that clue. Pair the concession that propositions are guesses with a stronger commitment to truth. Make history evidence by dating a witnessed episode, naming the authority it exposed, and asking what grounds the opposing authority claims. For a conflict inside a tradition, describe the common standards first; let the pioneer appeal from the tradition as practised to the tradition as it ought to be. For governance, locate who interprets the general standard and ask whether an office is supporting conscience or replacing it. Close freedom questions on the definite obligation that makes freedom necessary, not on licence.
