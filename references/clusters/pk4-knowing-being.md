# Personal Knowledge, Part Four: Knowing and Being (chs. 11–13) [pk11–pk13]

**Register.** The book turns from epistemology to ontology and cosmology. Prose warms; the final
chapter rises to open peroration. This is the ontological twin of Part One: as anti-objectivism
refused to let explicit rules exhaust knowing, anti-reductionism here refuses to let the lower level
exhaust the higher.

**Ch. 11, The Logic of Achievement.** Machines and organisms are defined by *operational
principles*, not by physics and chemistry. A machine's principle is a "rule of rightness" — it
tells how the thing works *when it works*, and so admits categories physics does not possess:
success and failure, function and malfunction. "Technology comprises all acknowledged operational
principles and endorses the purposes which they serve"; the operational principle "functions as an
ideal: the ideal of the machine in good working order," setting "a standard of perfection," as
"geometrical crystallography ignores the imperfections of crystals." Knowing a machine means
knowing what it is *for*; a complete physical-chemical description of a clock would never yield the
concept "clock." Animal learning (tricks, sign-reading, way-finding) prefigures contriving,
observing, reasoning — engineering, natural science, mathematics on the articulate level.

**Ch. 12, Knowing Life.** Biology is an *appraisal of achievement*: to recognize an organism is to
recognize a centre of norms it can meet or fail. Physiognomic and functional knowing are irreducibly
from-subsidiary-to-focal. Biology "gives way to a two-levelled structure" — but not the two levels
of observing dead matter (observer above, object below). By extending attention to "active beings
centred on themselves," three levels emerged; these collapse into two "representing the outlook of
man centring on things higher than himself." Man "stand[s] on the lower level of this commitment"
— "the personal pole of a commitment of which the ideals of man form the universal pole." Knowing
life thus rejoins the commitment framework of ch. 10.

**Ch. 13, The Rise of Man.** An emergent cosmology. Evolution read not as the shuffling of
chromosomes but as the coming-into-being of centres of responsible choice — he presses the
morphogenetic-field puzzle (regeneration and specialization proceeding while chromosomes merely
duplicate) to argue "there is some fundamental principle missing here," an ordering principle above
molecular control. A footnote forecloses the usual escape: quantum indeterminacy gives only "a
certain range of random variations which would be strictly unaccountable," and "human judgment is
anything but a strictly unaccountable random choice," so a quantum automaton represents mind no
better than a mechanical one. The book ends in open confession: "we know man to be the most precious
fruit of creation — and hence also the knowledge of this fact lies outside natural science. For we
possess this knowledge only by our submission to a firmament of obligations to which we believe all
human beings to be equally subject." The rise of man "extends... far beyond biology, into our
acceptance of what we believe to be man's nature and destiny." Teilhard's noosphere is entertained;
the "firmament of self-set standards" is traced back to worms.

**Moves to reproduce.** Ask of any system what its *operational principle* is before asking its
physics; treat success/failure as evidence of an irreducible level; convert "is X reducible to Y"
into "does Y possess the categories under which X is even described"; end an argument by naming the
firmament of obligations rather than by closing it.

**Load for:** philosophy-of-biology, emergence, reductionism/mechanism, mind–body, AI-as-mind
questions, teleology, evolution-and-meaning, and the ontological grounding of human dignity.
