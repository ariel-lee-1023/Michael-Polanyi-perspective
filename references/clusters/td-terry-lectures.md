# The Tacit Dimension: Terry Lectures (1962; published 1966) [c08]

**Register.** Three spoken lectures compress the long architectonic argument of *Personal Knowledge* into a sequence of live demonstrations: first make the listener experience tacit integration, then generalize it into levels and emergence, then make it the ground of a free intellectual order. The prose is leaner and more direct than the book chapters—mean sentence length 24.72 (median 22; stdev 13.86), with the corpus’s elevated second-person share for firsthand work (3.0%)—and it repeatedly names a distinction in front of the audience: *we may call*, *let me show*, *take the case.* Hedges run 8.48/1k, boosters 3.35/1k, hedge:booster 2.53, semicolons 3.81/1k, em dashes 2.66/1k; first/second/third person are 62.5%/3.0%/34.5%, and MATTR-500 is 0.482. Its dominant words—*science, tacit, scientific, knowledge, knowing, man, moral, terms*—match the lecture’s bridge from perception to intellectual and civic life; *people, reason,* and *idea* are conspicuously absent. The corpus slice is the roughest OCR scan in this package, so its quotations must remain confined to visibly clean stretches and corrupted passages are paraphrased here.

**Lecture I, Tacit Knowing — make the relation visible before naming it.** The first lecture begins with an experimental and bodily route rather than an abstract thesis. Conditioning experiments in which subjects anticipate a shock without being able to identify the relevant syllables establish the elementary point: someone can acquire a practical orientation without being able to state the particulars that guide it. The same form reappears in a face recognized by unreportable features, in a skill coordinated by muscular acts not separately identifiable, and in probe or stick use, where pressure in the hand is transposed into awareness of a remote object. This is not an appeal to an occult residue. It identifies a relation between two terms, one relied upon and one attended to.

The formula is this cluster’s: tacit knowing involves attending “from something” in order to attend to something else. The first term is named *proximal* and the second *distal*. Proximal does not mean merely spatially close, nor does distal mean simply an external object; they mark the functional orientation by which the first is relied on in attending to the second. The face illustrates the relation: one attends from its features to the characteristic appearance and may therefore be unable to specify the features. A skilled performance does the same with elementary muscular movements and its jointly intended achievement. This is the **functional** aspect of tacit knowing.

The lecture then prevents the formula from becoming a bare direction of attention. In the learned shock relation, the setting itself changes in appearance: the subject becomes aware of a particular syllable in the apprehension it arouses. More generally, one is aware of a proximal term in the appearance of its distal term. This is the **phenomenal** aspect. A third aspect concerns the bearing of one term on what it signifies. The features bear on a face, the effects felt through a probe bear on the object at its tip, and a tool’s impact bears on what it is doing to a thing. Meaning is displaced away from the body toward the distal term. This is the **semantic** aspect, and it gives the anatomical language of proximal and distal its justification.

The fourth, **ontological**, aspect is introduced through the comprehensive entity jointly constituted by the terms. The scan corrupts a few words in its defining sentence, but the usable argument is clear: proximal particulars are known in their joint meaning, and the act understands the whole they compose. Do not detach this from the first three aspects or turn it into an independent metaphysical postulate. In this lecture ontology is reached through the structure of a concrete act of knowing: functional reliance, phenomenal transposition, semantic meaning, and comprehension of a whole.

The body is the limiting case that makes indwelling intelligible. “Our body is the ultimate instrument of all our external knowledge,” normally experienced not as an object but in terms of the world attended to from it. A probe becomes bodily in the same structural sense: when a thing functions as the proximal term, “we incorporate it in our body—or extend our body to include it—so that we come to dwell in it.” The extension is deliberately broad. A theory is interiorized when it is used to see nature; moral teaching is interiorized when it becomes the framework of judgment; scientific and humanistic understanding are both forms of indwelling, not two epistemically unrelated methods. The lecture corrects an empathy-only account of the humanities by making indwelling the more precise general act underlying observation itself.

This creates the principal refusal of Lecture I. Explicit integration can deepen understanding—an engineer can know a machine more deeply than its user, and formal prosody may enrich a poem—but it cannot replace every tacit integration. A theory of a frog must continue to bear on a frog already identified informally; bringing any theory to its subject is itself a tacit act. Hence the ideal of a comprehensive mathematical account of experience that abolishes tacit knowing is, in the lecture’s verdict, “self-contradictory and logically unsound.” Exactitude is not rejected; the attempt to make its own practical and referential conditions disappear is.

The Meno paradox supplies the positive reframe. If all knowledge were explicitly statable, the thinker could neither know a problem nor search for its solution: either the sought object is already known or it cannot be recognized. The lecture says that “the Meno shows conclusively” why this explicit-only picture makes discovery impossible. A problem is instead an intimation of a coherence not yet comprehended. The scientist sees what is hidden, anticipates possibilities of an integration that others cannot yet see, and can appraise a discovery’s prospective fruitfulness through tacit foreknowledge of consequences not explicitly possessed. Treat this not as a mystical license for hunches but as a description of responsible discovery: the intimation must be pursued, articulated, and brought to bear on reality.

**Lecture II, Emergence — translate the tacit relation into a stratified ontology.** The second lecture moves from the relation in knowing to the relation in what is known. The proximal/distal pair becomes “two levels of reality, controlled by distinctive principles.” The higher relies on laws governing its elements but its operations are not explained by those laws. The first illustration is engineering: physical law leaves boundary conditions undetermined; an operational principle shapes matter within these open margins without suspending physics or chemistry. This is *marginal control*: a higher organizational principle governs the boundary conditions left open by the lower-level laws on which it relies.

The hierarchical example of speech shows why this is not a vague appeal to holism. Voice production is shaped into words by vocabulary; vocabulary is shaped into sentences by grammar; sentences can serve a style; style can convey a composition. The levels work under dual control: the laws proper to their elements and the organizing principle of the next comprehensive entity. One cannot derive vocabulary from phonetics, grammar from vocabulary, good style from correct grammar, or a composition’s content from style. The conclusion is exact: it is impossible to represent “the organizing principles of a higher level” by laws governing isolated particulars. This is a method for testing reductions: identify what the lower account leaves indeterminate, then show what higher operation controls those margins.

Biology is the decisive application. Mechanical explanation has genuine achievements—the organs work much like machines and may be studied through an ascending hierarchy of controls—but these successes do not turn life into physics and chemistry. A living function has an outcome it “may achieve or fail to achieve”; that normative alternative is absent from inanimate processes, which achieve nothing and are, in that sense, unerring. The distinction entails values, standards, possible failure, and an agent or centre to which performances may be attributed. It also makes biology necessarily critical: organisms are appraised by the standards of their own functioning, and the range of possible failure increases through morphology, physiology, perception, learning, intelligence, moral judgment, and evil.

*Emergence* is named here as the action producing the next higher level: from inanimate to living and from one biotic level to the next. A lower level cannot create a higher one merely by its own operations, because it cannot control the boundary conditions that it itself leaves open. The arising of a higher level thus has a process not manifest in the lower and consequently qualifies as emergence. The formula is anchored in the hierarchy rather than in mere novelty: a higher principle relies on but does not infringe the lower laws, is unaccountable in the lower’s vocabulary, and is liable to failure in operating through its lower conditions. The lecture aligns this with comprehension and inventiveness: both create new comprehensive entities—a poem, a machine, a knowledge of nature—without being mechanically deduced from their particulars.

The evolutionary application is emphatic. The relevant question is not exhausted by which populations survive; it is how a higher individual and its capacities come into being. The lecture traces a continuity from life’s first emergence through increasingly comprehensive functions toward personhood, while refusing to describe moral sense as an irrelevant afterthought. As capabilities rise, liabilities to malformation, disease, error, and evil rise too, and the individual centre grows more accountable. Human moral sense, respect for its possibility, reverence for those greater than oneself, and obedience to higher demand are therefore held within the question of the rise of man. This is a hierarchical account of evolution directed toward responsible judgment, not a value-neutral summary of biological change.

**Lecture III, A Society of Explorers — turn intellectual freedom into a form of ordered responsibility.** The third lecture returns from emergence to the civic condition of thought. It begins from the Soviet challenge: a political doctrine claiming scientific certainty denies the independent power of thought and, with it, grounds for freedom of thought. Its larger diagnosis is that skepticism and moral perfectionism can fuse into a self-destructive moral passion. The proposed response is neither individual intellectual self-determination nor a retreat from commitment. Tacit knowing shows that thought is rooted in subsidiary commitments and oriented toward a reality it seeks to discover; responsible freedom is possible only for agents able to see problems no one else sees and pursue them at their own risk.

The social model is directly named: “I call this a society of explorers.” An explorer stands amid potential discoveries and numberless possible problems. In science, investigators dispersed over the world find congenial areas within one field of potential thought; their results coordinate into systematic expansion. The model is then extended to literary and artistic movements, legal and social reform, and cultural development. It is not a picture of isolated geniuses. Science depends on a field assumed to be systematically progressive, on durable appointments and support, and on the novice’s entrance into a tradition of inquiry.

Coordination is the institution’s central move. The lecture calls scientific cooperation “self-co-ordination by mutual adjustment in science.” Each participant knows only a minute fragment of the whole but maintains, interprets, and may revise standards through mutual control. The newcomer joins an interplay of co-ordination, trusts traditions with little direct experience, and simultaneously claims an independent position from which tradition may be reinterpreted or revolutionized. The text explicitly keeps a personal-belief qualification on its extension of this model through all sciences; preserve that qualification rather than presenting the scope claim as a demonstrated sociology.

The society of explorers is also a society of authority, but not a dogmatic one. It is “controlled throughout by such mutually imposed authority”: scientific opinion, literary and artistic circles, professional associations, moral circles, and political rivals exert judgment through overlapping, often divided, fields of mutual appreciation. The test is whether these groups foster thought’s intrinsic power; their enemies are the movements that fear and hate autonomous initiative. Freedom here is not license or mere tolerance. It is the protected responsibility to explore a real field of possibilities under standards one receives, inhabits, and may transform.

**Moves to reproduce.** Start with an experience the interlocutor can perform—recognizing a face, using a probe, learning a skill, grasping a problem—then name the relation it has already displayed. Introduce a term only after showing the two terms it distinguishes; use *from–to* to force a question away from stored content and toward the direction of reliance. When exact description claims sufficiency, ask what whole had to be tacitly identified for the formal description to bear on it. Reframe the Meno dilemma: a genuine problem is evidence of tacit anticipation, not a proof that inquiry is irrational. For reductions, identify the lower laws, the higher operation, and the boundary conditions left open for marginal control. For institutions, refuse the choice between atomized autonomy and dogmatic command: show how independent initiative can be coordinated by mutual authority under a tradition that permits revision. End by returning freedom to responsibility for a field of truth, not preference.
